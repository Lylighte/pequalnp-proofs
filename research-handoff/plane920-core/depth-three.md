# Depth-three AND/OR circuits on the heavy-input domain need 39 gates

Depth counts physical gate levels on an input-to-output path. Each raw input has depth zero. The result allows arbitrary sharing at each level and free constants. It suffices that each designated output has a representation of depth at most three within the same circuit.

Use the dual predicates on zero sets Z of size at most four. A wire is active when the original wire is false. A valid output for a line L is active exactly on Z=L. Raw point p is active if p belongs to Z. An accepting tree takes one active child at a dual OR and all children at a dual AND. Its support is the set of active raw points encountered. Identically true leaves have empty support. By monotonicity the support alone activates the wire, and so does any larger admissible set.

Choose for each line a gate of minimum depth computing its target on the promised domain, breaking ties by gate index. It is a binary dual AND. No raw input, invalid reference, constant or unary gate needs all four points. If a dual OR computed the target, an active child at L would itself compute that target because the OR is inactive everywhere else. Both parents of the selected AND are active on L, and each has depth at most two. If an AND has the same child twice, the child already computes the output.

There are two ways to assign two distinct earlier gates to this line.

1. If some accepting tree of a parent on L has at least three distinct raw points, that parent must be a dual AND. A depth-two OR can select only one child of depth at most one. The AND's two children also have depth at most one, so at least one child contributes two distinct points in that accepting tree. This child is the dual AND of those two raw points. The child and its parent are active on L, and activity of either requires both points. Since two distinct points determine a unique line, neither gate is active on any other target line.
2. Otherwise, choose accepting trees on L for its two parents f,g, with respective supports A,B. Both have at most two distinct points. Their union activates the output, so A and B must be disjoint pairs covering L. If f is active on any S of size at most two, then f and g are active on the allowed set S ∪ B. Hence S ∪ B=L, which forces S=A. Thus f has the unique accepted set A among all sets of size at most two. Likewise g has the unique accepted set B. These gates are distinct because A and B differ. Neither can be a raw input, a constant, or an invalid reference, as such a wire cannot have a unique accepted pair and no accepted singleton.

Gates assigned by the first rule can never be assigned to a different line by either rule, since every gate assigned to a line is active on that line. For a gate assigned twice using the second rule, its unique accepted pair would have to belong to both lines, which is impossible. The thirteen output gates are distinct and none can be an assigned parent or grandchild of any other line: each output is active at only its own line. The two earlier gates and selected output for every line give 39 distinct physical gates.

The counting argument also applies without the depth hypothesis whenever every line has a pair of small parent certificates as in rule 2 or two distinct earlier gates active only at that line's target row as in rule 1. The geometric and functional parts of the argument are checked in DepthLabels.lean. It proves label uniqueness, the forced pair on a small accepting parent, and the global injection count.
